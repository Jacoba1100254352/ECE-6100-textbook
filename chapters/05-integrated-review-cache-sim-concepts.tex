\chapter{Integrated Synthesis and Cache Simulation Concepts}\label{ch:auto-006}

A simulator reports fewer cache misses after a change, yet the modeled program runs more slowly.
That result is possible: the larger cache may take longer to access, or the new policy may generate expensive traffic elsewhere.
To explain it, we need to follow an event through the whole machine instead of treating each counter as a separate verdict.


\section{From an Event to an Explanation}\label{sec:auto-046}
Begin with one demand access.
Ask which level receives it, whether a miss causes a fill or write-back, and which counters record each event.
Then ask whether the resulting delay actually stalls the processor or overlaps other work.
This sequence connects the cache model to runtime and prevents a common mistake: interpreting more activity as more useful work.


\section{Integrated Themes from Chapters 1 and 2}\label{sec:auto-047}
The first two chapters establish the measurement framework used throughout the rest of the book.
An architectural change should first be classified by what it is trying to improve: parallelism, speculation, or some combination of the two.
Its performance impact must then be evaluated with the correct metric, usually runtime for one job or throughput for a stream of jobs.
The central CPU-time equation,
\[
    \text{CPU time}=\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}\times\text{CT},
\]
turns that reasoning into a concrete tradeoff among instruction count, pipeline efficiency, and clock period.
Within that framework, Amdahl's law bounds fixed-size speedup, Gustafson's law explains scaled-size behavior, and the choice between arithmetic and harmonic means determines whether a summary is mathematically meaningful.

\paragraph{Worked check}\label{par:auto-026}
Consider an optimization that accelerates 30\% of runtime by $2\times$.
The total speedup is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-045}
    S=\frac{1}{(1-0.3)+\frac{0.3}{2}}=\frac{1}{0.85}\approx 1.176.
\end{equation}
The unaccelerated fraction remains in the denominator and bounds overall speedup.

\paragraph{Worked check: maximum possible speedup}\label{par:auto-026a}
Consider a mechanism that accelerates 25\% of the original benchmark runtime and makes that portion arbitrarily fast.
The maximum total speedup is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-045a}
    S_{\max}=\frac{1}{1-0.25}=\frac{4}{3}\approx 1.33.
\end{equation}
This is the standard way to interpret the \emph{maximum} speedup of a partial optimization.


\section{Integrated Themes from Chapter 3}\label{sec:auto-048}
Chapter 3 adds the memory-hierarchy side of the performance model.
That means translating a cache specification such as $(C,B,S)$ into sets, ways, and tag/index/offset fields; distinguishing compulsory, conflict, and capacity misses; and understanding how replacement and write policies alter observed traffic.
It also means using
\[
    AAT=HT+MR\cdot MP
\]
as a compact summary of hierarchy behavior while remembering that cache size, block size, and associativity can influence all three terms in different ways.

\paragraph{Worked check}\label{par:auto-027}
Consider a cache with $HT=1.8$ ns, $MR=0.06$, and $MP=28$ ns (Eq.~\eqref{eq:auto-046}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-046}
    AAT = 1.8 + 0.06\cdot 28 = 3.48\ \text{ns}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Worked check: repeated trace reasoning}\label{par:auto-027a}
Consider a direct-mapped L1 with $(C,B,S)=(8,4,0)$, write-back/write-allocate, and the repeated access sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-046a}
    R\ 0x41F1,\ R\ 0xB54A,\ W\ 0x4204,\ W\ 0xCA3B,\ R\ 0x420E,\ W\ 0xB537.
\end{equation}
With $B=4$, the six accesses touch the five blocks $\{0x41F,0xB54,0x420,0xCA3,0xB53\}$ because $0x4204$ and $0x420E$ lie in the same block.
The first iteration therefore has 5 compulsory misses.
The key structural fact is that blocks $0xCA3$ and $0xB53$ map to the same direct-mapped set, so after warm-up the sequence continues to suffer two conflict misses per iteration.
Over iterations 2--10, that produces 18 additional misses.
Those 18 misses are the non-compulsory misses for the full run, and because the conflicting writes repeatedly evict dirty lines, the full sequence produces 19 writebacks if dirty lines are \emph{not} flushed after the final access.

\paragraph{Victim cache and +1 prefetch contrast}\label{par:auto-027b}
That same trace separates two common remedies cleanly.
A small victim cache can eliminate the recurring two-block ping-pong at one set; after warm-up, the combined L1+victim-cache miss count can drop to zero because each conflicting block is recovered from the victim structure instead of DRAM\@.
By contrast, a $+1$ prefetcher is not automatically a cure.
In this trace it can prefetch block $0x420$ early enough to remove one first-iteration demand miss, but it does not remove the recurring conflict between $0xCA3$ and $0xB53$.
This is the key design lesson of the trace: next-block prefetching and victim caching address different miss mechanisms.


\section{Integrated Themes from Chapter 4}\label{sec:auto-049}
Chapter 4 connects pipeline structure to observed performance.
Reading a timing diagram is therefore an exercise in inference: the reader is reconstructing hazards, bypass paths, memory behavior, and branch-handling policy from visible stage occupancy.
Static direction baselines include always taken and backward taken / forward not taken.
One-bit and Smith-counter schemes, GShare, GSelect, local-history predictors, perceptrons, and tournament predictors add runtime learning.

\subsection*{Pipeline Speedup}\label{subsec:auto-012}
Assume a 5-stage design where each stage has 250 ps logic delay and pipeline-register overhead is 30 ps.
Unpipelined instruction time is $5\times250=1250$ ps.
Pipelined clock is $250+30=280$ ps.
Ideal long-run speedup is (Eq.~\eqref{eq:auto-047}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-047}
    S\approx\frac{1250}{280}\approx 4.46.
\end{equation}
This is below 5 because register overhead is non-zero.

\subsection*{CPI with Branch Stalls}\label{subsec:auto-013}
If branch frequency is 18\% and each branch contributes an average of 1.6 stall cycles (Eq.~\eqref{eq:auto-048}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-048}
    CPI = 1 + 0.18\times1.6 = 1.288.
\end{equation}
This illustrates how even moderate branch penalties raise CPI above the ideal value of 1.

\subsection*{Integrated CPI Accounting}\label{subsec:auto-013b}
Suppose a scalar core has base $CPI=1$.
Loads are 30\% of all instructions, the L1 data-cache miss ratio on loads is 5\%, and each such miss adds 20 cycles.
Branches are 18\% of all instructions, the misprediction rate is 10\%, and each misprediction adds 2 cycles.
Assume the stated penalties are additional to base execution and do not overlap one another.
Then the added CPI from load misses is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-048a}
    0.30\times 0.05\times 20 = 0.30,
\end{equation}
while the added CPI from branch mispredictions is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-048b}
    0.18\times 0.10\times 2 = 0.036.
\end{equation}
So the total CPI estimate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-048c}
    CPI \approx 1 + 0.30 + 0.036 = 1.336.
\end{equation}
This kind of combined accounting is the most useful way to compare bottlenecks: all stall mechanisms are translated into the common unit of cycles per retired instruction.

\subsection*{Software Branch Handling}\label{subsec:auto-013a}
When a branch has delay slots, every instruction moved into those slots must be safe regardless of whether the branch is taken.
When a branch instead has squash slots under an ``assume taken'' policy, the compiler may place instructions from the predicted taken path there, because wrong-path entries will be canceled.
The resulting placement rule is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item fill delay slots only with path-independent instructions,
    \item fill squash slots from the predicted path when profitable, and
    \item leave \texttt{NOP}s when correctness would otherwise be uncertain.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Cache Simulation Conceptual Model}\label{sec:auto-050}
The cache simulation framework models hierarchy behavior at the level of addresses, tags, and policy events rather than payload data values.\footnote{Optional coding companion: BitLemon, \href{https://youtu.be/ss-PDXKKjH8?t=300}{\emph{Cache Simulator in C++: Building a Basic Framework}} (05:00, cache structures). Its implementation is a separate model; compare event definitions before transferring counts between simulators.}
A common parameterization is (Eq.~\eqref{eq:auto-049}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-049}
    (C_1,B,S_1,C_2,S_2,\{\text{MIP,LIP}\},\{\text{None,+1,Markov,Hybrid}\},r).
\end{equation}
In this notation, $C$ controls capacity ($2^C$ bytes), $B$ controls block size ($2^B$ bytes), and $S$ controls associativity ($2^S$ ways).
Here \textit{most recently used insertion policy (MIP)} and \textit{least recently used insertion policy (LIP)} are common insertion-policy options.


\section{Hierarchy Semantics in the Model}\label{sec:auto-051}
The hierarchy model treats L1 accesses separately from L2 internals, while L2 requests are viewed as arriving from an upper-level requester.
This separation makes it easier to reason about statistics at each level without conflating local policy with downstream timing.

Write strategy also changes the meaning of those statistics.
\textit{Write-back, write-allocate (WBWA)} behavior in L1 emphasizes dirty-state tracking and deferred propagation, whereas \textit{write-through, write-no-allocate (WTWNA)} behavior in L2 forwards every write to the next level and does not allocate on write misses.
Forwarding a write does not imply that another processor can already observe it or that it has reached persistent storage.

The ordering of read-miss repair and writeback events can also matter.
Different servicing orders change replacement-state evolution and therefore alter downstream hit, miss, and writeback counts.
A disabled cache level requires an explicit modeling rule.
If requests bypass it entirely, its lookup contributes no hit time; substitute the next active level's service time directly.
If the specification instead models an always-miss lookup stage, retain only the latency that specification assigns to it.


\section{Prefetching Concepts}\label{sec:auto-052}
\textit{$+1$ prefetching} predicts the next sequential block ($X+1$) after a demand miss on block $X$.
\textit{Markov prefetching} instead models miss-stream transitions as a finite-state table, associates each source miss block with a bounded set of likely successors and their frequencies, and selects a prediction from the highest-frequency successor under a deterministic tie-break rule.
\textit{Hybrid prefetching} combines correlation-based prediction with sequential fallback when correlation information is absent.

To interpret prefetch behavior correctly, the simulator must also distinguish demand-filled lines from prefetched lines.
A prefetch metadata bit provides that distinction.
In this model, the first demand use of a prefetched line counts as a prefetch hit, while eviction before first demand use counts as a prefetch miss.
These are usefulness counters; another simulator may use the same names for lookup outcomes instead.

\paragraph{Worked prefetch-counter interpretation}\label{par:auto-027c}
Suppose a simulator reports 120 prefetched lines, 70 prefetch hits, and 25 prefetched lines evicted before first use.
The 70 prefetch hits show that many speculative fills were in fact useful: later demand accesses avoided ordinary misses because of earlier prefetching.
The 25 unused evictions show that some prefetched lines consumed bandwidth and space without helping demand accesses.
The remaining prefetched lines are ambiguous without more context, because some may still be resident and not yet demanded.
This is why prefetch usefulness is not judged by one counter alone; both benefit and waste must be read together.


\section{Statistics and AAT Interpretation}\label{sec:auto-053}
Typical hierarchy statistics include access counts, hit/miss counts, hit/miss ratios, writeback counts, and prefetch effectiveness counters.\footnote{Optional coding companion: BitLemon, \href{https://youtu.be/y9xDHGzSAyg?t=80}{\emph{Cache Simulator in C++: Measuring Cache Hit Rate}} (01:20, access/hit counters; 03:40, sequential test).}
In two-level analysis (Eq.~\eqref{eq:auto-050}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-050}
    AAT_{L1}=HT_{L1}+MR_{L1}\cdot MP_{L1},\qquad MP_{L1}=AAT_{L2}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-051}
    AAT_{L2}=HT_{L2}+MR_{L2,read}\cdot DRAM\_TIME.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:auto-052}
    DRAM\_TIME = DRAM\_AT + DRAM\_AT\_PER\_WORD\cdot WORDS\_PER\_BLOCK.
\end{equation}
Here $DRAM\_AT$ is startup time before the per-word transfers.
It differs from $T_1$ in Chapter 3's transfer model, which already includes delivery of the first sub-block.
That definition explains why one formula multiplies by the full word count and the other uses $N_{sb}-1$ follow-up transfers.
This form emphasizes that higher-level penalty is the lower-level average access behavior, not a fixed constant.


\section{Conceptual Tradeoffs Across Parameters}\label{sec:auto-054}
Increasing $C$ generally reduces capacity misses but tends to raise hit time.
Increasing $S$ reduces conflict misses but increases lookup complexity.
Increasing $B$ can exploit spatial locality but may also increase pollution and miss penalty.
More aggressive prefetching can reduce compulsory misses yet increase bandwidth pressure and useless fills.


\section{What to Carry Forward}\label{sec:auto-055}
A useful simulator report tells a causal story: a demand access misses, a fill or write-back changes the hierarchy, and some part of the resulting delay becomes lost processor progress.
Check the denominator, the event definition, and the timing assumptions before comparing any two counters.
A lower miss ratio is evidence about one part of that story, not a complete performance verdict.

This method extends to the queues and reorder buffers in the next chapter.
The names of the structures change, but the question remains where useful work waits and what prevents it from moving.
Appendix~\ref{ch:formula-compendium} collects the formulas once those modeling choices are clear.


\section{Practice Questions}\label{sec:ch5-practice}
The following questions reinforce the integrated ideas of Chapter 5 and are intended for self-checking after the summary.
\begin{enumerate}[beginpenalty=10000]
    \item A hierarchy reports the following parameters:
    \[
        HT_{L1}=1,\quad MR_{L1}=0.10,\quad HT_{L2}=7,\quad MR_{L2,read}=0.20,\quad \text{DRAM\_TIME}=80.
    \]
    Compute $AAT_{L2}$ and $AAT_{L1}$.
    Then explain why the L1 miss penalty is modeled as the lower-level \emph{average} access time rather than as a fixed constant.
    \item A simulator reports 120 prefetched lines, 70 prefetch hits, and 25 prefetched lines evicted before first use.
    What do these three counters say about prefetch usefulness and prefetch waste?
    What additional information would you want before concluding that the prefetcher is a net win?
    \item In a hierarchy that uses write-through, write-no-allocate behavior at the lower level, why is it often more meaningful to report the lower-level miss ratio over \emph{reads only} rather than over all accesses?
    \item Two cache configurations have the same capacity and associativity.
    Configuration A has lower hit time but higher miss ratio.
    Configuration B has higher hit time but lower miss ratio.
    Explain why the better design cannot be chosen correctly without computing or estimating average access time.
    \item Explain why the servicing order of read-miss repair and writeback traffic can change later hierarchy statistics even when the high-level policy choices are unchanged.
    \item A scalar core has base $CPI=1$.
    Loads are 30\% of all instructions, the L1 data-cache miss ratio on loads is 5\%, and each such miss adds 20 stall cycles.
    Branches are 18\% of all instructions, the branch misprediction rate is 10\%, and each misprediction adds 2 cycles.
    Estimate the total CPI and identify which source of stalls contributes more.
\end{enumerate}
