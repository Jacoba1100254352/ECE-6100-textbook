\chapter{Lecture Sources and Historical Notes}\label{ch:source-notes}
The course materials supplied with this project are the primary reference for its scope and terminology.
Slide numbers refer to the printed identifiers inside the handouts, which may differ from PDF page numbers because a PDF page can contain two slides.

\section*{How Attribution Works}
Lecture examples are paraphrased, not reconstructed quotations.
They preserve the teaching point while making modeling assumptions explicit.
Examples added to connect ideas, rather than recovered from a lecture, are presented as the book's own illustrations.

\section*{Primary Research and Historical References}
\begin{description}
  \item[Multicore and energy efficiency.] Intel's \href{https://www.intel.com/pressroom/archive/releases/2006/20060307corp.htm}{March 7, 2006 Core microarchitecture introduction} documents the contemporary emphasis on multicore performance and energy efficiency. It provides historical context for the transistor-budget discussion in Chapter 1.
  \item[Vector registers and chaining.] Cray Research, \emph{CRAY-1 Computer Systems Hardware Reference Manual}, HR-0004F, May 1982, sections on vector registers and chaining. The manual describes eight vector registers with up to 64 elements each and explains the conditions for forwarding an element stream between functional units. Available from the Computer History Museum's \href{https://bitsavers.computerhistory.org/pdf/cray/CRAY-1/HR-0004F_CRAY-1_Computer_Systems_Hardware_Reference_Manual_May82.pdf}{Bitsavers archive}.
  \item[Dynamic scheduling.] R. M. Tomasulo, \emph{An Efficient Algorithm for Exploiting Multiple Arithmetic Units}, IBM Journal of Research and Development, 11(1), 1967, pp.\ 25--33. This is the original description of the Model 91 floating-point scheduling method. \href{https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.111.0025}{doi:10.1147/rd.111.0025}.
  \item[Two-level predictor organizations.] Tse-Yu Yeh and Yale N. Patt, \emph{Alternative Implementations of Two-Level Adaptive Branch Prediction}, ISCA, 1992. The \href{https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~kubitron/courses/cs252-S09/handouts/papers/p451-yeh.pdf}{paper} distinguishes global and per-address histories and shared versus separate pattern tables. Chapter 4 identifies its local-history example within that larger family.
  \item[Perceptron predictors.] Daniel A. Jiménez and Calvin Lin, \emph{Dynamic Branch Prediction with Perceptrons}, HPCA, 2001. The \href{https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~lin/papers/hpca01.pdf}{authors' paper}, Sections 3 and 5, supplies the training rule, linear-separability limitation, and empirically tuned history-length threshold used in Chapter~4.
  \item[Modulo scheduling.] B. Ramakrishna Rau, \emph{Iterative Modulo Scheduling: An Algorithm for Software Pipelining Loops}, MICRO, 1994, pp.\ 63--74. The \href{https://doi.org/10.1145/192724.192731}{paper} distinguishes the initiation-interval lower bound from a constructed legal schedule and describes bounded scheduling attempts. Texas Instruments' \href{https://software-dl.ti.com/codegen/docs/c7000/optimization_guide/5_understand_opt/compopt_under_pipelining.html}{C7000 optimization guide} illustrates pipeline startup, steady state, draining, and register-allocation constraints in a production compiler.
  \item[Network resource dependencies.] William J. Dally and Charles L. Seitz, \emph{Deadlock Free Message Routing in Multiprocessor Interconnection Networks}, Caltech technical report, 1986, available through \href{https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/vqrey-g1h07}{CaltechAUTHORS}. Chapter 10 uses its channel-dependency perspective rather than treating the presence of virtual channels as a proof of deadlock freedom.
  \item[Cedar under real traffic.] Josep Torrellas and Zheng Zhang, \emph{The Performance of the Cedar Multistage Switching Network}, IEEE Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems 8(4), 1997, pp.\ 321--336, \href{https://doi.org/10.1109/71.588598}{doi:10.1109/71.588598}. Hardware-probe measurements on a 16-vector-processor configuration identify traffic convergence and unequal contention delays as important limitations; the book does not transfer those results to every Cedar configuration.
  \item[Noisy quantum devices.] John Preskill, \emph{Quantum Computing in the NISQ Era and Beyond}, Quantum 2, 79, 2018; \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.00862}{author manuscript}. This supplies the historical device-regime terminology, not a fixed prediction about every later platform.
  \item[Quantum query models.] John Watrous's \href{https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/fundamentals-of-quantum-algorithms/quantum-query-algorithms/introduction}{IBM Quantum course} provides the query-model context. The book derives its one-qubit $HZH$ identity and Deutsch comparison directly; an oracle-query advantage does not automatically establish a whole-program speedup.
  \item[SYNDRA, STRIDE, and SUP.] Pulkit Gupta, \emph{Architectures and Primitives for Composable Superconducting Supercomputers}, \href{https://grad.gatech.edu/events/phd-proposal-pulkit-gupta}{Georgia Tech Ph.D. proposal announcement}, May 5, 2026. The abstract verifies the names and high-level architectural goals; the April 23 recording supplies the mechanisms discussed here. Appendix D's diagrams and one-wide barrel numbers remain explanatory models, not a full implementation specification or a verified hardware benchmark.
  \item[Refrigeration cost.] Ray Radebaugh, \emph{Refrigeration for Superconductors}, \href{https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=50016}{NIST author copy}, Section II. The Carnot coefficient of performance gives the ideal cooling-work bound used in Appendix D; actual equipment has additional losses.
\end{description}

\section*{Specifications Behind Implementation Caveats}
\begin{description}
  \item[Memory placement.] The Linux kernel's \href{https://docs.kernel.org/mm/numa.html}{NUMA documentation} explains local allocation and the cost of remote placement and migration. Chapter 8 distinguishes this physical organization from the shared-memory programming model.
  \item[Dirty shared data.] AMD's \emph{Software Optimization Guide for the AMD Family 10h and 12h Processors}, publication 40546, section on \href{https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/archived-tech-docs/software-optimization-guides/40546.pdf}{MOESI and cache-line ownership}, describes an $O$ owner with $S$ sharers while memory can be stale. This is the distinction drawn in Chapters 8 and 9; state names alone are not a complete protocol specification.
  \item[Barriers and language-level ordering.] Mellor-Crummey and Scott's \href{https://www.cs.rochester.edu/research/synchronization/pseudocode/ss.html}{scalable-synchronization pseudocode} includes the sense-reversing centralized barrier. Chapter 9 spells out acquire/release orders rather than treating the original abstract pseudocode as ordinary C/C++ accesses. The C++ working draft defines \href{https://eel.is/c++draft/intro.races}{data races and happens-before} and \href{https://eel.is/c++draft/atomics.order}{atomic synchronization orders}; its language rules are separate from a hardware-only litmus model.
  \item[Transactional fallback.] Intel's \href{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/tsx-anti-patterns-in-lock-elision-code.html}{lock-elision anti-patterns} explain why the transactional path must read the fallback lock. Its \href{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/community/tsx-fallback-paths.html}{fallback discussion} also emphasizes that best-effort hardware transactions need a nontransactional path.
  \item[Renamed state and retirement.] The RISC-V BOOM implementation documentation distinguishes \href{https://docs.boom-core.org/en/latest/sections/rename-stage.html}{explicit physical-register storage from data-in-ROB storage}, explains \href{https://docs.boom-core.org/en/latest/sections/reg-file-bypass-network.html}{operand-read ports and bypassing}, and describes \href{https://docs.boom-core.org/en/latest/sections/reorder-buffer.html}{retirement and committed-store draining}. Chapter 6 uses these concrete designs to avoid treating every renamed core as a future file with a separate committed data copy.
  \item[Atomic operations and reservations.] The \href{https://docs.riscv.org/reference/isa/unpriv/a-st-ext.html}{RISC-V atomic-extension specification} provides a concrete example of selectable acquire/release ordering and permitted store-conditional failure. These details motivate the distinction between atomicity and ordering in Chapter~\ref{ch:sync-consistency}; other ISAs have their own rules.
  \item[Dynamic and guided loop scheduling.] The \href{https://www.openmp.org/spec-html/5.2/openmpse66.html}{OpenMP worksharing-loop specification} distinguishes fixed-size dynamic chunks from guided chunks that decrease as work is consumed. Chapter~\ref{ch:sync-consistency} uses this distinction when describing a fetch-and-add work counter.
  \item[Persistence and cached writes.] Intel's \href{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/enabling-persistent-memory-in-the-storage-performance-development-kit-spdk.html}{persistent-memory programming discussion} explains why writes can remain in volatile caches or buffers and need appropriate propagation to reach a persistence domain. Chapter 3 separates this requirement from write-through policy and cache coherence.
  \item[Translation faults.] The \href{https://docs.riscv.org/reference/isa/priv/supervisor.html}{RISC-V supervisor-level specification}, under virtual-address translation, illustrates that page-table validity and permission checks can raise faults. Chapter 3 distinguishes a cached-translation miss from a page fault and from storage I/O.
\end{description}
